# Interpreting a Survey

A survey can describe what its respondents reported, but the interpretation depends on who took part and how the questions were asked. An association between two answers does not, by itself, demonstrate that one behavior caused the other. This distinction should guide the reading of research on study habits.

Suppose students who report a regular study schedule also report feeling more prepared for examinations. One possible explanation is that the schedule improves preparation. Another is that students with fewer outside demands have more time both to plan and to study. The survey alone would not resolve these alternatives.

In addition, the words used in a question can change its meaning. “Regular study” might mean a fixed daily routine to one respondent and a recurring weekly session to another. Therefore, any comparison should examine the study's definitions, its sampling method, and the uncertainty of its estimates. A source still needs to be selected and read before this hypothetical example can be replaced with a research finding.
